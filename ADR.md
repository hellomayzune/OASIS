# 🗺️ OASIS — Architecture Decision Records

This document catalogs the primary architectural and design decisions governing the **OASIS** (Overheating Assessment through Screening-Level Intervention Scenarios) notebook suite, structured in accordance with a lightweight **MADR** (Markdown Architectural Decision Records) framework. Each record systematically details the operational **Context**, the formal **Decision**, the evaluated **Alternatives considered**, and the resulting technical **Consequences**. General, repository-wide architectural decisions are presented first, followed sequentially by stage-specific decision records corresponding to individual notebooks across the pipeline.

# 🌐 Cross-cutting decisions

### ADR-001 — Separate Evidence Layers

**Context.**

Empirical monitoring, physics-based dynamic simulation, and data-driven predictive modeling address distinct research questions. When these evidence streams are blended, strong performance in one layer can create a false impression of validity in another.

**Decision.**

OASIS strictly segregates four distinct evidence layers across its pipeline: *observe* (Stages 1–2), *explain physically* (Stages 3A–3B), *benchmark and diagnose prediction* (Stages 4–5), and *stress-test and screen* (Stages 5–6). Each sequential stage ingests, but never overwrites or alters, the output files generated by preceding stages.

**Alternatives considered.**

Developing a single, monolithic end-to-end notebook that integrates measurement, dynamic simulation, and predictive forecasting was evaluated and rejected, as this approach obscures whether specific findings originate from empirical measurements, physics-based simulations, or surrogate projections.

**Consequences.**

This architecture increases the overall number of notebooks and intermediate hand-off files. However, it ensures complete auditability, allowing every reported metric or screening outcome to be traced directly back to its underlying evidence layer.

---

### ADR-002 — `1_input/` → `2_output/` Directory Structure Convention

**Context.**

The execution workflow spans seven interconnected notebooks that share primary raw data files and progressively pass intermediate data products down the pipeline to subsequent analytical stages.

**Decision.**

All notebooks strictly ingest raw data from `1_input/` along with intermediate outputs generated by earlier stages, and write analytical outputs exclusively to `2_output/`. Output files from Stages 1 through 3B reside directly at the root of `2_output/`, whereas output files from Stages 4 through 6 are organized into `tables/`, `figures/`, and `models/` subdirectories. Files located within `1_input/` are treated as immutable and are never modified by any script.

**Alternatives considered.**

Appending calculated results directly back into the primary input Excel workbooks or establishing isolated per-notebook working directories were evaluated and rejected, as both approaches obscure file provenance and make identifying the authoritative data source ambiguous.

**Consequences.**

This strict directory separation allows any notebook to be re-run from a clean kernel once the outputs of its upstream stages exist, and establishes transparent, file-path-based hand-offs between pipeline stages.

---

### ADR-003 — Generating Reported Values Dynamically From Saved Tables

**Context.**

Manually transcribing numerical results into documentation text introduces a risk of stale or inconsistent values whenever upstream data pipelines or notebooks are re-executed on updated datasets.

**Decision.**

All analytical summaries, findings, and diagnostic statements rendered within notebooks compute their values dynamically from the output tables generated during the current execution run. Markdown cells are reserved exclusively for describing methodology, operational context, and interpretation, omitting hardcoded result values.

**Alternatives considered.**

Hardcoding headline metrics directly into markdown cells was evaluated and rejected, as it represents a primary source of statistical drift and divergence across pipeline execution runs.

**Consequences.**

Specific numerical results, statistical bounds, and empirical findings must be retrieved directly from the regenerated files within `2_output/` following a clean pipeline run.

---

### ADR-004 — Explicit Separation of Screening Metrics from Regulatory Compliance

**Context.**

Metrics such as cumulative hours exceeding 26 °C, cooling degree-hours (CDH), the defined risk tiers (Low, Elevated, High, and Severe), and surrogate confidence flags carry a risk of being misinterpreted as official regulatory compliance criteria.

**Decision.**

Every screening metric is explicitly designated as a study-specific screening construct at its point of introduction within Stage 5 (Steps 5.2–5.3) and Stage 6 (Section 6.3), ensuring a clear distinction from CIBSE TM59 and Building Regulations Approved Document O. Furthermore, any TM59-style indicators computed using surrogate or simulated outputs are explicitly labelled to reflect their derived nature.

**Alternatives considered.**

Adopting conventional pass-or-fail terminology for the screening tiers was evaluated and rejected, as it blurs exploratory screening outputs with formal compliance outcomes.

**Consequences.**

While this requires more precise and descriptive labeling throughout the documentation and figures, it ensures that no analytical output can be misconstrued as a regulatory compliance certificate.

---

### ADR-005 — Dynamic Derivation of Inputs and Settings from Data

**Context.**

As long-term environmental monitoring continues, sensor installation dates vary across rooms, and monitored room configurations may evolve over time. Hardcoding these parameters creates maintenance overhead and fragility.

**Decision.**

Within Stages 2, 4 and 5, all workbook files matching the pattern `NottinghamMeasured*.xlsx` are discovered and dynamically merged on the `Time` index, with precedence assigned to the most recently modified file in the event of timestamp collisions. The header row is detected automatically on each sheet, and room identifiers, spatial categories, floor levels, and compass orientations are parsed programmatically from sheet nomenclature (such as `Kitchen_NW` or `Bed4_SE`). Operational windows—including active monitoring years, partial cooling seasons, training and evaluation splits, heatwave analysis periods, and representative baseline seasons—are computed directly from the loaded data streams.

**Alternatives considered.**

Hardcoding static room lists, calendar dates, and train-test split years within the code was evaluated and rejected, as this approach would mandate manual code modifications with every subsequent data update.

**Consequences.**

Newly deposited workbooks are integrated automatically without requiring script alterations, though newly introduced room configurations must be explicitly registered within the global `ROOM_TYPES` definition dictionary. The sole exception is the Stage 1 MIDAS comparison, which ingests a single explicitly named workbook (`OASIS_XLSX`) alongside a fixed list of indoor rooms possessing complete records dating from May 2023 (`INDOOR_ROOMS`), both of which are defined within its initial configuration cell.

---

### ADR-047 — Withholding Raw Monitoring Records While Releasing Code and Processed Outputs

**Context.**

The primary monitoring dataset originates from an occupied private residential dwelling, and the meteorological boundary conditions rely on third-party data subject to strict licensing and distribution terms.

**Decision.**

The repository openly distributes the analytical codebase under the MIT Licence, alongside the EnergyPlus simulation workbooks and all derived data products, tables, figures, and serialized model bundles within `2_output/` under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International license (CC BY 4.0). The raw monitoring workbook is withheld from public distribution to honor participant confidentiality agreements and safeguard occupant privacy. Similarly, DSY weather files and Met Office MIDAS Open observations are not redistributed, requiring users to acquire them directly from the COLBE and CEDA platforms, respectively. The exact structural schema of each withheld input file is comprehensively documented within `DATASHEET.md`.

**Alternatives considered.**

Publishing an anonymized version of the raw monitoring record with metadata removed was evaluated and rejected, as high-resolution hourly room-level time series from a single residential property cannot be fully anonymized against re-identification. Redistributing the third-party weather files directly within the repository was also rejected in favor of directing users to the original providers' authorized access routes and terms.

**Consequences.**

Pipeline stages 3A, 3B, and 6 can be fully executed and re-run using the public files combined with independently downloaded weather data. However, stages 1, 2, 4, and 5 require access to the private monitoring workbook or an alternative dataset structured to identical specifications; consequently, their empirical execution outputs are preserved and provided as pre-computed, saved deliverables within the repository.

# 📍 Stage 1 — Outdoor record check against MIDAS

### ADR-046 — Benchmarking the On-Site Outdoor Sensor Against a Reference Met Office Station

**Context.**

The on-site outdoor temperature sensor directly drives the calculation of the exponentially weighted running mean outdoor temperature, which governs the adaptive thermal comfort boundaries defined in CIBSE TM59 Criterion (a). However, local microclimates, sensor placement, and solar shielding effects may cause deviations from standard meteorological weather station measurements.

**Decision.**

The on-site `External` temperature series is benchmarked against hourly MIDAS Open observations recorded at the Sutton Bonington Met Office station. Cross-correlation analysis automatically detects and applies the optimal temporal lag (`ALIGN_LAG_H = "auto"`). Statistical comparisons report hourly performance under both raw time-stamped and lag-aligned conditions, accompanied by bias uncertainty intervals estimated via a 7-day moving-block bootstrap. Daily mean calculations mandate a minimum completeness threshold of 20 valid hourly readings (`MIN_HOURS_DAY`). To ensure a rigorous like-for-like evaluation, the MIDAS running mean is initialized on the same calendar date as the measured series, discarding the initial 28 days as a warm-up period (`WARMUP_DAYS`), while a parallel full-history MIDAS running mean quantifies initialization warm-up effects. Finally, the downstream impact of these temperature discrepancies on EN 16798-1 (Categories I–III) and ASHRAE Standard 55 adaptive comfort boundaries—and their corresponding indoor comfort-hour counts—is evaluated across all indoor zones with complete records originating from May 2023.

**Alternatives considered.**

Replacing the on-site outdoor monitoring series entirely with regional station data was evaluated and rejected, because the on-site record provides an authentic representation of the microclimatic boundary conditions experienced directly by the building envelope.

**Consequences.**

This strategy quantifies systemic sensor bias and isolates its precise numerical influence on the adaptive limit that sets CIBSE TM59 Criterion (a). However, because only two time series are evaluated, microclimatic site effects cannot be formally decoupled from sensor-shielding effects.

# 📍 Stage 2 — Empirical overheating assessment

### ADR-006 — Application of CIBSE TM59 Criteria Directly to Monitored Room Temperatures

**Context.**

CIBSE TM59 is conventionally established as a design-stage assessment methodology evaluated against physics-based simulation outputs rather than empirical indoor monitoring data.

**Decision.**

Stage 2 evaluates CIBSE TM59:2017 Criterion (a) alongside TM59:2026 Criteria (b) and (d) directly using monitored indoor room temperature series. Consequently, the resulting thermal comfort evaluations implicitly capture the household's actual real-world operational behaviors, including physical window operation, solar shading usage, and natural ventilation patterns. No artificial ventilation-restriction adjustments or idealized behavioral assumptions are applied.

**Alternatives considered.**

Simulating an idealized design-stage representation of the dwelling to derive conventional TM59 compliance metrics was evaluated and rejected, as this approach discards the authentic occupant operational dynamics that the empirical assessment seeks to evaluate.

**Consequences.**

The computed compliance metrics reflect true as-occupied thermal performance rather than theoretical design intent. Consequently, these empirical results cannot be directly compared against traditional design-stage TM59 simulation reports for the same property.

---

### ADR-007 — Exclusion of Missing Observations from Exceedance Numerators and Denominators

**Context.**

Standardizing thermal comfort accounting requires a consistent protocol for handling missing data points; treating unrecorded hours as compliant by default introduces systemic downward bias in calculated exceedance rates.

**Decision.**

For CIBSE TM59 Criteria (a) and (d), any hour missing either an indoor or an outdoor temperature reading is excluded from both the cumulative exceedance hour count (numerator) and the total occupied hours base (denominator). For Criterion (b), a nocturnal sleeping period (23:00–08:00) is evaluated only if a minimum of 6 out of the 9 required hourly observations are present; unevaluated nights are tracked and reported separately. Any cooling season containing fewer than 95% valid recorded hours is formally flagged as a partial monitoring season.

**Alternatives considered.**

Assigning missing observations a default compliant status was evaluated and rejected, as this approach artificially understates thermal exceedance frequencies across monitoring periods containing data gaps.

**Consequences.**

Exceedance percentages remain directly comparable across room-years possessing varying degrees of data completeness. However, room-years affected by late sensor installations or temporary dropouts operate on a smaller total sample base.

---

### ADR-008 — Selection of CIBSE TM59 Category II Thermal Thresholds

**Context.**

CIBSE TM59 establishes distinct thermal comfort assessment thresholds based on occupant vulnerability, defining Category I for sensitive or vulnerable populations and Category II for standard residential occupancy.

**Decision.**

The assessment framework configures standard occupancy limits (`CATEGORY = "II"`), applying an upper adaptive temperature limit of $T_{\text{max}} = T_{\text{comf}} + 3\text{ K}$ for living rooms, kitchens and bedrooms under Criterion (a), a fixed sleeping-period threshold of $27\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ under Criterion (b), and a maximum allowable limit of 4 exceedance nights per cooling season.

**Alternatives considered.**

Applying Category I vulnerability criteria was evaluated and rejected, as the monitored case study dwelling represents a standard, non-vulnerable residential household.

**Consequences.**

Configuring the classification level relies on a single configurable parameter within the codebase. Adopting Category I criteria instead would impose more stringent limits under Criteria (a) and (b), giving a stricter result.

---

### ADR-009 — Reporting CIBSE TM59 Criterion (d) for Internal Circulation Spaces as a Risk Flag

**Context.**

Under CIBSE TM59:2026, Criterion (d) provides a pass or fail evaluation for communal circulation spaces shared between distinct residential units. In contrast, the monitored Hall and Landing are internal circulation spaces within a single dwelling.

**Decision.**

Hours exceeding $28\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ in the Hall and Landing between 1 May and 30 September are evaluated over 24 hours against the 3% exceedance limit. These results are formally reported as elevated thermal risk flags rather than statutory pass or fail determinations.

**Alternatives considered.**

Excluding circulation areas entirely from the evaluation pipeline was evaluated and rejected, as continuous environmental measurements in these central spaces show the vertical temperature gradient within the dwelling.

**Consequences.**

Evaluating circulation zones highlights internal heat distribution patterns and thermal risk; however, these outputs are interpreted strictly as indicative diagnostic indicators rather than formal regulatory compliance outcomes.

---

### ADR-010 — Air Temperature as a Proxy for Operative Temperature with an Operative-Offset Estimate

**Context.**

CIBSE TM59 compliance criteria are formally defined using operative temperature, whereas the deployed environmental loggers measured dry-bulb air temperature directly, with mean radiant temperature left unrecorded.

**Decision.**

Dry-bulb air temperature is used as the primary analytical variable across the assessment pipeline. A steady-state surface heat balance calculation—incorporating an internal surface resistance of 0.13 m²K/W, as-built envelope U-values, assumed radiant-view factors, and the solar heat absorbed by the inner pane—bounds the estimated operative-minus-air temperature offset across distinct sunlit daytime and nocturnal conditions. TM59 thermal comfort outcomes are subsequently recomputed with these estimated thermal offsets applied.

**Alternatives considered.**

Treating air temperature as equivalent to operative temperature without verification, or alternatively implementing a fully dynamic indoor radiant exchange model. The first approach leaves the underlying thermal bias unquantified, while the second requires detailed surface-temperature inputs and empirical radiative parameters that were not captured during monitoring.

**Consequences.**

The magnitude and directional bias introduced by substituting air temperature for operative temperature are explicitly quantified for each evaluation criterion. However, this steady-state offset estimation framework necessarily excludes direct shortwave solar radiation incident on occupants and transient heat releases from internal structural thermal mass.

---

### ADR-011 — Continuous Calculation of Outdoor Running Mean Temperature

**Context.**

Calculating the exponentially weighted outdoor running mean temperature ($\alpha = 0.8$) required for adaptive thermal comfort limits under CIBSE TM59 Criterion (a) requires an adequate historical lead-in period to establish stable initial values.

**Decision.**

The outdoor running mean temperature ($T_{\text{rm}}$) is computed continuously across the multi-year empirical dataset rather than being re-initialized at the start of each cooling season. Operational bounds of $10\text{ }^\circ\text{C} \le T_{\text{rm}} \le 30\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ are strictly enforced in accordance with standard adaptive comfort formulations.

**Alternatives considered.**

Re-initializing $T_{\text{rm}}$ on 1 May at the start of each calendar evaluation season was evaluated and rejected, as early-season assessment days would suffer from truncated historical memory and inaccurate running mean estimates.

**Consequences.**

Adaptive comfort thresholds applied during early-season warm periods draw upon the maximum possible continuous historical temperature memory. This approach depends on uninterrupted multi-year outdoor temperature records to avoid synthetic initialization artifacts.

---

### ADR-012 — Selection of a Representative Complete Season for Diurnal Heatmap Visualizations

**Context.**

Constructing a two-dimensional day-by-hour diurnal thermal matrix requires a continuous, unbroken seasonal data record, whereas the most recently logged monitoring period may remain partially incomplete at the time of analysis.

**Decision.**

Diurnal heatmap visualisations select the most recent complete cooling season extending through 30 September. Partial or ongoing monitoring seasons are excluded from primary heatmap rendering to preserve matrix structural integrity.

**Alternatives considered.**

Plotting the most recent monitoring season regardless of missing tail-end data was evaluated and rejected, as incomplete temporal ranges truncate the visualization layout and distort seasonal thermal trends.

**Consequences.**

Diurnal heatmap figures display the most recent complete seasonal dataset available, which may trail the latest active, partially recorded monitoring year.

# 📍 Stage 3A — Physics-model check against measurements

### ADR-013 — Empirical Verification of External Dynamic Simulation Outputs Without In-Pipeline Simulation Execution

**Context.**

The primary physics-based dynamic building thermal model was developed in EnergyPlus and DesignBuilder externally to this analytical repository.

**Decision.**

Stage 3A is restricted to computing statistical agreement metrics and generating comparative diagnostic figures directly from supplied paired measured and simulated time series. Input auditing against physical as-built documentation is detailed within notebook markdown cells without re-executing thermal simulations within the automated pipeline.

**Alternatives considered.**

Re-implementing the full dynamic physics-based building model within Python was evaluated and rejected, as it falls outside the project scope and is unnecessary for checking simulation output against empirical measurements.

**Consequences.**

All agreement statistics and diagnostic figures are fully reproducible within this repository using the provided output files. However, re-executing the underlying physics-based thermal simulation requires the external EnergyPlus modeling environment.

---

### ADR-014 — Terminology Restricted to "Checked Against Measurements" Rather Than "Validated"

**Context.**

The only extended monitoring interval featuring concurrent indoor measurements and zero dwelling occupancy occurred between 10 July and 30 July 2024, during which all operable windows remained continuously closed.

**Decision.**

The dynamic thermal model is explicitly described as having been checked against measurements during an unoccupied baseline period. This comparison evaluates fabric conduction and solar gain responses under controlled conditions, deliberately excluding occupant heat gains, window ventilation operations, and extreme heatwave performance. Standard validation terminology is avoided.

**Alternatives considered.**

Designating the model as fully validated based on achieving standard hourly error tolerances was evaluated and rejected, because conventional calibration tolerances were formulated for building energy-use calibration rather than short-term free-floating thermal performance under a single static operational state.

**Consequences.**

Intervention scenario results generated in Stage 3B are interpreted strictly as comparative evaluations under standardized operational assumptions, rather than absolute predictive forecasts of real-world thermal behavior.

---

### ADR-015 — Prioritisation of Absolute Error Metrics in Kelvin Over Normalised Indices for Hourly Simulation Checking

**Context.**

Established calibration guidelines such as CIBSE TM63 and ASHRAE Guideline 14 specify percentage-based hourly tolerance criteria, specifically Coefficient of Variation of Root Mean Square Error (CV(RMSE)) below 30% and Normalized Mean Bias Error (NMBE) within plus or minus 10%. Because these indices are normalized against the mean indoor temperature measured in degrees Celsius, they can produce misleadingly favorable evaluations when applied to bounded indoor dry-bulb temperature ranges.

**Decision.**

While standardized normalized indices are reported for transparency, Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) evaluated in kelvin are established as the primary quantitative measures of simulation agreement, supplemented by coefficient of determination values ($R^2$) calculated independently for each thermal zone.

**Alternatives considered.**

Relying on monthly aggregated error tolerances or depending solely on conventional normalized percentage indices were evaluated and rejected, as these metrics obscure high-frequency thermal deviations that are critical for assessing hourly residential overheating performance.

**Consequences.**

Simulation agreement can be evaluated directly against the $1\text{ K}$ operational precision margin inherent in CIBSE TM59 thermal comfort compliance thresholds.

---

### ADR-016 — Independent Per-Zone Regression Fits and Moving-Block Bootstrap Confidence Intervals

**Context.**

Applying a pooled regression fit across all building zones can obscure localized thermal dynamics and mask individual zone divergences, while high-frequency hourly temperature residuals exhibit significant temporal autocorrelation.

**Decision.**

Independent regression models and associated statistical metrics are computed separately for each individual thermal zone. Statistical uncertainty intervals are constructed using a moving-block bootstrap methodology utilizing 24-hour blocks (`BOOT_BLOCK_H = 24`), which is consistently applied across both Stage 4 and Stage 5 analyses.

**Alternatives considered.**

Employing a pooled cross-zone regression fit or applying a standard row-wise independent bootstrap was evaluated and rejected, as these approaches understate zone-specific thermal behavior divergences and fail to account for serial temporal autocorrelation, respectively.

**Consequences.**

Zone-level thermal anomalies remain clearly identifiable in the reported results, and the resulting confidence intervals are statistically more robust and defensible against autocorrelation biases.

---

### ADR-017 — Verification Restricted to Five Monitored Zones

**Context.**

The model verification output workbook supplied for Stage 3A contains simulated temperature time series covering five specific physical zones within the dwelling.

**Decision.**

Model checking against empirical measurements is conducted exclusively for the Kitchen, Living Room, Master Bedroom, Bedroom 4, and Bedroom 3. The Hall and Landing circulation spaces are omitted from this physics-based validation step.

**Alternatives considered.**

Re-running external simulation models to generate dedicated output time series for internal circulation spaces was evaluated and rejected, as executing new physical simulations falls outside the scope established for Stage 3A.

**Consequences.**

Physics-based verification metrics for the Hall and Landing are not available within Stage 3A outputs. Where Passive and Hybrid offsets are needed for these spaces in Stage 5, they take the mean profile of the simulated rooms on the same floor (ADR-036).

# 📍 Stage 3B — Simulated scenarios under future weather

### ADR-018 — Adoption of Design Summer Year (DSY) Weather Series for Outdoor Boundary Conditions on an Hour-Beginning Index

**Context.**

The external scenario evaluation workbook contains an unpopulated `Outdoor` temperature column, while the official Design Summer Year (DSY) weather datasets are timestamped on an hour-ending basis (hours 1 through 24) across a single representative year.

**Decision.**

Outdoor dry-bulb temperature boundary conditions are supplied directly from the DSY2030 and DSY2080 weather files, with the dwelling orientation assessment sheet utilizing the 2030 dataset. Following verification of the underlying calendar sequence, all DSY records are aligned to an hour-beginning continuous datetime index established on a non-leap reference year.

**Alternatives considered.**

Attempting to extract outdoor temperatures from the unpopulated workbook column or treating timestamps as arbitrary categorical variables were both evaluated and rejected, as a continuous datetime index is strictly required to calculate outdoor running mean temperatures and apply seasonal masking filters.

**Consequences.**

Outdoor running mean temperatures ($T_{\text{rm}}$) and corresponding adaptive comfort thresholds are computed directly from the external DSY weather files. The assigned non-leap reference year serves exclusively as a temporal indexing convention and does not alter the underlying meteorological data.

---

### ADR-019 — Centralisation of Nocturnal Exceedance Temperature Thresholds

**Context.**

CIBSE TM59 Criterion (b) applies a fixed $27\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ threshold to the mean temperature of the sleeping period (23:00–08:00) under Category II.

**Decision.**

The nocturnal temperature threshold is encapsulated within a single configuration variable (`NIGHT_THRESHOLD_C`) and consumed exclusively by a central criterion evaluation function within the codebase.

**Alternatives considered.**

Hard-coding the $27\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ value across multiple evaluation modules was evaluated and rejected, as distributed numerical literals increase maintenance complexity and introduce risk of parameter inconsistency across calculation stages.

**Consequences.**

Modifying nocturnal thermal limits or adapting the pipeline for alternative occupancy classifications requires updating a single parameter, ensuring consistent application throughout Stage 3B.

---

### ADR-020 — Preservation of As-Built Zone Identifiers Across Dwelling Orientation Sensitivity Runs

**Context.**

Sensitivity analysis of dwelling orientation involves rotating the entire building geometry across cardinal directions, while individual output data columns retain their baseline, as-built spatial identifiers (e.g., `KITCHEN_NW`).

**Decision.**

Individual thermal zones retain their static as-built room identifiers across all rotational permutations. Spatial orientation for a given simulation run is defined exclusively by the overarching dataset or sheet group designation (North, East, South, West).

**Alternatives considered.**

Dynamically relabeling individual room tags to reflect their post-rotation cardinal orientations was evaluated and rejected, as changing spatial identifiers across simulation runs obscures zone-by-zone comparisons across different rotational scenarios.

**Consequences.**

Comparative evaluation across rotational scenarios requires cross-referencing static zone identifiers alongside the corresponding scenario rotation label.

# 📍 Stage 4 — ML surrogate, benchmarks and SHAP

### ADR-021 — Chronological Per-Room Train-Test Data Partitioning

**Context.**

Environmental sensors were deployed across different dates, and random train-test splitting strategies introduce temporal data leakage between adjacent hourly observations.

**Decision.**

Data splitting is executed independently for each individual thermal zone using a chronological approach. A complete monitoring year is defined from 1 May through 30 April, counting as complete when it holds at least `COMPLETE_YEAR_HOURS` feature rows. The test set for each room starts at the last complete monitoring year and includes any later partial monitoring year.

**Alternatives considered.**

Applying a unified calendar cut-off date across all rooms or utilizing random train-test splitting methodologies were evaluated and rejected, as the first approach wastes valuable monitoring data, while the second introduces severe temporal leakage between correlated sequential hours.

**Consequences.**

Train-test boundary dates vary slightly between individual rooms and will advance automatically as new empirical monitoring data are ingested into the repository.

---

### ADR-022 — Uniform Hyperparameter Configuration Across All Thermal Zones

**Context.**

Employing individual per-room hyperparameter tuning increases the risk of model overfitting and confounds comparative evaluations between distinct thermal zones.

**Decision.**

A single unified hyperparameter configuration (`max_depth = 6`, `n_estimators = 600`, `learning_rate = 0.03`, `subsample = 0.8`, `colsample_bytree = 0.8`, `reg_lambda = 1.0`) is applied across all thermal zones. This configuration was selected via a four-point grid search restricted to a chronological validation slice derived solely from the training years of the upper-floor reference room, and subsequently applied universally. The identical hyperparameter configuration is utilized for the temperature-change ($\Delta T$) model, with its corresponding grid search results documented separately.

**Alternatives considered.**

Performing independent per-room hyperparameter optimization was evaluated and rejected, as the primary research objective is to evaluate the generalizable information content of the model class rather than maximizing individual room-specific curve-fitting accuracy.

**Consequences.**

Comparative analyses between different rooms and model formulations remain free from hyperparameter-induced confounding effects, and held-out test years are strictly protected from selection bias.

---

### ADR-023 — Comprehensive Benchmarking of Machine Learning Surrogates Against Persistence, Linear, and Grey-Box Baselines

**Context.**

Indoor dry-bulb temperature exhibits strong temporal autocorrelation, meaning that high coefficient of determination ($R^2$) values can potentially arise solely from the previous hour's measured thermal state rather than genuine predictive model capability.

**Decision.**

The predictive performance of each thermal zone's XGBoost surrogate model is evaluated across identical held-out test hours against multiple rigorous baseline formulations: immediate persistence ($t - 1$), diurnal persistence ($t - 24$), a ridge autoregressive exogenous (ARX) linear model utilising identical feature sets, a single-node resistance-capacitance (1R1C) grey-box model with daily and annual harmonics representing gains, and a weather-only XGBoost model. Performance differentials are assessed using a paired moving-block bootstrap test, and the derived 1R1C model parameters provide an explicit effective thermal time constant.

**Alternatives considered.**

Relying exclusively on immediate persistence benchmarks or depending solely on summary $R^2$ and root mean square error metrics was evaluated and rejected, as these simplified metrics fail to demonstrate whether non-linear machine learning architectures provide genuine informational value over straightforward physical or linear baseline formulations.

**Consequences.**

The utility of the machine learning surrogate is rigorously benchmarked against the strongest available alternative methods, and the extracted thermal time constant offers a direct physical interpretation of the observed indoor temperature persistence patterns.

---

### ADR-024 — Segregation of One-Step and Recursive Multi-Step Performance Metrics in Surrogate Evaluation

**Context.**

Feeding measured previous-hour indoor temperatures directly into regression models creates a one-step-ahead prediction structure that can conflate short-term persistence with genuine multi-step forecasting capability.

**Decision.**

One-step evaluation metrics are explicitly designated as state-conditioned performance measures. Multi-step recursive forecasting performance—where model predictions are fed back iteratively as lagged inputs—is evaluated independently over the hottest 14-day window of the held-out period (the heatwave window) against a held-seed persistence baseline, as well as across weekly rolling forecast origins spanning the held-out cooling seasons utilising 168-hour prediction horizons with bootstrap resampling across origins.

**Alternatives considered.**

Relying exclusively on one-step performance metrics or evaluating recursive performance during a single isolated heatwave period were evaluated and rejected, as these approaches fail to measure generalisable projection skill across diverse meteorological conditions.

**Consequences.**

Model predictive rankings are established across distinct temporal horizons, and surrogate reliability under future weather boundary conditions is judged from rigorous multi-step recursive evidence.

---

### ADR-025 — Explicit Evaluation of Model Extrapolation Boundaries Under Elevated Thermal Conditions

**Context.**

Tree-based ensemble regression algorithms are inherently constrained in their capacity to extrapolate predictions beyond the maximum numerical range of their training targets, while empirical observations confirm that subsequent test years experienced higher ambient temperatures than the baseline training period.

**Decision.**

One-step prediction errors are explicitly evaluated and reported for held-out operating hours where the preceding hour's indoor temperature exceeds the training ninety-fifth percentile and, separately, where it exceeds the absolute training maximum. Furthermore, model systematic bias is quantified across discrete outdoor dry-bulb temperature bins spanning less than 15 °C, 15 to 20 °C, 20 to 25 °C, 25 to 30 °C, and greater than or equal to 30 °C.

**Alternatives considered.**

Relying exclusively on aggregate summary error metrics across the entire dataset was evaluated and rejected, as global aggregation obscures significant predictive failures and error concentrations during extreme warm-weather hours, which represent the critical operational domain for residential overheating assessment.

**Consequences.**

The underlying structural mechanisms governing model performance limitations during extreme thermal events are tested and documented directly, eliminating the need to infer extrapolation boundaries from aggregated performance figures.

### ADR-026 — Implementation of Temperature-Change XGBoost as a Controlled Alternative to Level Models

**Context.**

Tree-based ensemble regression algorithms predicting absolute temperature levels are inherently bounded by the maximum values present within their training targets, which can restrict performance when forecasting out-of-sample warming conditions.

**Decision.**

A temperature-change ($\Delta T$) XGBoost model is implemented to predict hourly temperature differentials using identical feature sets, training years, and hyperparameters as the baseline level model, with multi-step forecasts reconstructed by adding predicted changes to the preceding state. A fixed evaluation protocol—encompassing baseline comparators, forecast horizons ranging from 24 to 336 hours, extreme-heat subset evaluations, specific error metrics, and a decision rule requiring the 95 percent paired bootstrap interval of the root mean square error difference to lie strictly below zero—is established prior to execution and reported regardless of the outcome. The absolute level model remains the primary designated surrogate architecture.

**Alternatives considered.**

Replacing the absolute level model entirely with the $\Delta T$ formulation or performing independent hyperparameter tuning specifically for the change model were evaluated and rejected, as the comparative methodology requires isolating the mathematical effect of the prediction target from variations in learner architecture or hyperparameter selection.

**Consequences.**

The specific effect of the prediction target is successfully isolated from learner performance characteristics, and extended year-long free-running simulation tests are established for Stage 5 evaluation.

### ADR-027 — Application of Split-Conformal Prediction Intervals to Quantify Recursive Forecast Uncertainty

**Context.**

Point forecasts generated by machine learning surrogate models lack explicit quantification of predictive uncertainty, which limits their utility for risk-informed thermal comfort and overheating assessments.

**Decision.**

Split-conformal prediction intervals are constructed by fitting a dedicated calibration model—trained without the final available training year—to forecast that specific year's cooling season. The fifth and ninety-fifth percentiles of the resulting residual distributions calculated across discrete temporal horizon bands (1 to 24 hours, 25 to 72 hours, and 73 to 168 hours) establish nominal 90 percent prediction intervals, with empirical coverage rates subsequently verified against held-out rolling-origin forecasts.

**Alternatives considered.**

Employing computationally intensive bootstrapped model ensembles was evaluated and rejected, as this approach introduces substantial processing overhead while remaining fundamentally restricted by the limitations of the training distribution.

**Consequences.**

Empirical under-coverage identified during warmer test years explicitly highlights climatic conditions and thermal states that were absent from the historical calibration dataset.

### ADR-028 — Restriction of SHAP Interpretations to Model-Attributed Associations

**Context.**

Lagged and rolling outdoor temperature features exhibit high collinearity, and direct physical variables such as solar radiation gains, window operation states, and occupant heat inputs are omitted from the machine learning surrogate feature set.

**Decision.**

Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) outputs are explicitly designated and described as model-attributed contributions and statistical associations. Apparent non-linear shifts or slope changes identified within the attribution values are interpreted as characteristics of the fitted machine learning model rather than underlying physical phenomena, and comparative evaluations between bedrooms with opposing orientations are treated as exploratory contrasts.

**Alternatives considered.**

Interpreting top-ranked SHAP features as direct physical drivers of indoor thermal dynamics was evaluated and rejected, as establishing direct physical causality is unsupportable given the constraints and feature limitations of the model architecture.

**Consequences.**

SHAP analysis is deployed exclusively as a diagnostic tool for inspecting surrogate internal behavior and generating qualitative hypotheses, rather than functioning as definitive empirical evidence of physical causality.

### ADR-029 — Implementation of Feature Warm-Up Periods and Extrapolation Auditing for DSY Projections

**Context.**

Lagged and rolling meteorological features require a 48-hour historical initialization window, and future climate projection weather series can introduce boundary conditions that exceed the numerical range of the historical training data.

**Decision.**

Raw Design Summer Year (DSY) CSV files provide dry-bulb temperature and relative humidity, from which running mean outdoor temperature ($T_{\text{rm}}$) and the upper adaptive limit ($T_{\text{max}}$) are recomputed. The initial 48 hours of each projection series serve as a feature warm-up period and are excluded from summary statistics. Furthermore, hours in which any input feature falls outside its established training range are explicitly counted and reported for each room and climate horizon (DSY2030, DSY2080).

**Alternatives considered.**

Back-filling the initial 48-hour warm-up window using meteorological data from later periods within the same year was evaluated and rejected, as artificial data imputation introduces structural distortion into the temporal feature sequence.

**Consequences.**

Long-term projections are reported alongside their respective extrapolation shares, ensuring that DSY-driven outputs are interpreted strictly as indicative performance indicators of the surrogate model under future climate scenarios rather than definitive regulatory compliance outcomes.

### ADR-030 — Physics-Machine Learning Comparison as a Cross-Model Consistency Check

**Context.**

The EnergyPlus model incorporates standardized CIBSE TM59 occupancy profiles, internal heat gains, and scheduled window opening operations, whereas the machine learning surrogate model empirically reproduces the observed operation of the monitored household.

**Decision.**

Direct comparative evaluations between the physics-based simulation and the machine learning surrogate are conducted for all corresponding spatial zones present in both models, with `BED1` mapped directly to the Master Bedroom. Performance comparison is executed across cooling-season mean temperatures, peak temperatures, and TM59-style overheating indicators, with observed levels of agreement or disagreement reported independently for each room.

**Alternatives considered.**

Designating either the physics-based EnergyPlus model or the empirical machine learning surrogate as an absolute reference standard was evaluated and rejected, as neither framework has been empirically tested under the operational assumptions of the other.

**Consequences.**

Any divergence identified between the physics-based and machine learning outputs is documented explicitly as a structural model-form difference and is intentionally left unreconciled.

### ADR-031 — Standardised Model Bundling with Integrated Metadata and Portable Fallback Serialization

**Context.**

Downstream analytical pipelines in Stages 5 and 6 require access to the exact, immutable regression models fitted and evaluated during Stage 4 without modification or retraining drift.

**Decision.**

Each fitted room-level surrogate model is saved as a structured `joblib` bundle containing complete operational metadata, including feature ordering, target variables, data split parameters, training feature numerical ranges, hyperparameters, random seeds, and software library versions. In parallel, a version-independent JSON booster representation is stored as a portable fallback. An automated round-trip verification check is executed to confirm that reloaded model instances exactly reproduce prior test-set predictions.

**Alternatives considered.**

Re-fitting models independently within each analytical notebook was evaluated and rejected, as decentralized model estimation introduces a significant risk of silent divergence across pipeline stages.

**Consequences.**

Downstream workflow stages operate using identical, version-locked model assets, and integrated training data ranges enable automated flagging of out-of-range inference inputs.

# 📍 Stage 5 — Future-weather exposure and cooling-degree-hours

### ADR-032 — Temporal Separation of Model Validation and Final Production Refitting

**Context.**

While utilising the maximum available monitoring period yields the most robust projection model, fitting a machine learning surrogate on the complete dataset prior to evaluation creates a circular dependency that invalidates out-of-sample validation.

**Decision.**

The final 20 percent of each thermal zone's chronological record (`TRAIN_FRACTION = 0.80`) is withheld as a dedicated validation set. An evaluation surrogate is fitted exclusively on the initial 80 percent of the data and executed recursively across the held-out validation period, initialised from the final measured temperature state and compared against a held-seed persistence forecast. After validation, a distinct production surrogate model is fitted on the complete, uninterrupted historical record to generate final Design Summer Year (DSY) projections.

**Alternatives considered.**

Reporting in-sample performance metrics derived from models fitted on the full dataset was evaluated and rejected, as such metrics are circular and overstate predictive capability.

**Consequences.**

Two distinct model sets are maintained within the analytical workflow—Stage 4 evaluation models and Stage 5 production models—with subsequent Stage 6 analyses explicitly quantifying any operational differences between them.

---

### ADR-033 — Recursive Baseline Benchmarking and Projected Exposure Uncertainty Intervals on Shared Validation Splits

**Context.**

A held-seed persistence forecast provides a weak baseline reference for long-horizon projections, and projected overheating exposure requires explicit quantification of predictive uncertainty bounds.

**Decision.**

Recursive autoregressive exogenous (ARX) and single-node resistance-capacitance (1R1C) models are executed across the identical 80/20 train-test split using the same initialization seed as the primary machine learning surrogate. A dedicated split-conformal uncertainty interval for projected exposure is calibrated using residual errors derived exclusively from odd ISO weeks of the test cooling season and subsequently verified against even ISO weeks, with the resulting lower and upper offset values applied directly to the DSY projections.

**Alternatives considered.**

Reusing the Stage 4 horizon-band prediction intervals was evaluated and rejected, as those intervals do not account for cumulative error propagation during unconstrained, year-long free-running DSY projections.

**Consequences.**

Cumulative overheating exposure is reported alongside rigorous split-conformal uncertainty bounds, with intervals that reflect error distributions observed on measured data, not errors under conditions outside the record.

---

### ADR-034 — Year-Long Free-Running Simulation Testing of Temperature-Change XGBoost

**Context.**

Multi-step forecasting architectures that recursively accumulate predicted hourly temperature changes can suffer from unconstrained long-term drift over extended annual simulation horizons, a failure mode that standard short-horizon evaluation windows fail to capture.

**Decision.**

The temperature-change ($\Delta T$) XGBoost model is refitted on the standard 80 percent training partition and executed recursively through the remaining 20 percent validation period using identical initialization seeds as the absolute level model. A strict pre-established acceptance protocol requires that the root mean square error of the $\Delta T$ model does not exceed that of the level model—verified by ensuring the 95 percent paired bootstrap interval of the error difference does not lie entirely above zero—and mandates that monthly mean bias remains within $\pm 1.0\text{ K}$ (`DRIFT_LIMIT_K`).

**Alternatives considered.**

Extrapolating long-term stability characteristics directly from 336-hour horizon test results was evaluated and rejected, as cumulative temporal drift only manifests across extended continuous execution runs.

**Consequences.**

The $\Delta T$-XGBoost architecture is either formally accepted for long-term DSY projection comparison or restricted exclusively to shorter-horizon evaluations based on empirical performance against the defined stability criteria.

### ADR-035 — Circular Padding for Meteorological Feature Construction and Initial-Condition Sensitivity Analysis in DSY Projections

**Context.**

Design Summer Year weather files represent isolated annual meteorological sequences, and recursive indoor temperature projections require a defined initial indoor thermal state at the beginning of the simulation period.

**Decision.**

The raw DSY time series is circularly padded by a 48-hour historical window solely to enable the calculation of outdoor lag and rolling meteorological features, without implying physical thermal continuity with the preceding calendar year. Long-term projections are initialized using the historical January mean indoor temperature, and resulting thermal trajectories initiated from the January tenth-percentile, mean, and ninetieth-percentile seed temperatures are explicitly compared.

**Alternatives considered.**

Assuming that the initial thermal seed effect dissipates rapidly within the simulation was evaluated and rejected, as explicit sensitivity testing is required to verify whether initial condition memory persists into the cooling season.

**Consequences.**

The magnitude of initial condition influence on seasonal cooling performance results is explicitly quantified and reported rather than assumed away.

### ADR-036 — Application of Passive and Hybrid EnergyPlus Offsets as Cross-Model Intervention Sensitivity Experiments

**Context.**

Re-simulating physical building interventions under the observed operation of the monitored household is not possible within this workflow.

**Decision.**

For every individual thermal zone, climate horizon, and distinct month-by-hour-of-day pairing between May and September, a thermal offset is derived by subtracting the EnergyPlus Passive (Case 2A) or Hybrid (Case 3B) simulated temperature from the baseline as-built simulation temperature; this offset is then subtracted directly from the machine learning free-running projection. Hall and Landing circulation spaces use the mean offset profile of the simulated rooms on the same floor. This transfer assumes that simulated intervention reductions are approximately additive to surrogate projections and maintain validity when translated from CIBSE TM59 standardized occupancy to the household's observed operation.

**Alternatives considered.**

Applying constant, unvarying assumed temperature offsets was evaluated and rejected due to a lack of physical traceability to simulation mechanics, while developing a dedicated household-specific retrofit simulation model fell outside available project resources.

**Consequences.**

The resulting comparative metrics illustrate the sensitivity of the overheating screening methodology to an assumed physical intervention response rather than serving as direct empirical validation of retrofit performance.

### ADR-037 — Active Temperature Ceilings and Cooling Degree-Hours as a Cooling-Demand Proxy

**Context.**

A calibrated functional relationship linking indoor operative temperatures, explicit cooling loads, mechanical plant capacities, and energy consumption metrics is not available for the empirical case study dwelling.

**Decision.**

Active conditioning scenarios are evaluated using standardized indoor temperature ceilings defined by set points of 18, 20, 22, and 24 °C (`SET_POINTS = [18, 20, 22, 24]`). For each ceiling, the free-running hours above the ceiling and the cooling degree-hours ($^\circ\text{C}\cdot\text{h}$) are reported. Cooling degree-hours are strictly maintained as a temperature-based cooling-demand proxy and are not converted into estimated energy consumption, carbon emissions, or financial costs.

**Alternatives considered.**

Applying generalized, assumed system load conversion coefficients was evaluated and rejected to prevent introducing false numerical precision into uncalibrated active cooling estimates.

**Consequences.**

Active scenario outputs are restricted to comparative evaluations of cooling demand proxies across different temporal horizons and operational set point thresholds.

---

### ADR-038 — Comprehensive Sensitivity Analysis of Overheating Exposure Across Model Forms, Systematic Bias Corrections, and Threshold Variations

**Context.**

Projected overheating exposure estimates are inherently sensitive to surrogate model architecture selection, known model under-prediction tendencies during high-temperature extremes, and the choice of the standard 26 °C screening threshold.

**Decision.**

Design Summer Year overheating exposure metrics are recomputed using alternative baseline models—specifically weather-only XGBoost, $\Delta T$-XGBoost, ARX, and 1R1C formulations refitted on the complete historical record. Systematic recursive bias evaluated per outdoor temperature bin is subtracted from each room's projections, with bins containing fewer than minimum required test hours (`MIN_BIN_HOURS`) defaulting to the next cooler adjacent bin. Finally, cumulative exposure is recalculated across alternative screening thresholds spanning 25 to 28 °C.

**Alternatives considered.**

Reporting overheating exposure exclusively from the primary machine learning surrogate evaluated at the standard 26 °C threshold was evaluated and rejected, as single-model reporting conceals the degree to which final conclusions depend on structural modeling assumptions.

**Consequences.**

The direction, magnitude, and room-level distribution of projected thermal exposure changes can be rigorously evaluated against variations in model form, residual bias corrections, and threshold definitions.

# 📍 Stage 6 — Reliability, room-level synthesis and screening

### ADR-039 — Verification of Model Hand-Off Integrity Without Independent Re-Training

**Context.**

Independently re-deriving analytical results across Stages 4 and 5 introduces a significant risk of numerical divergence between pipeline components.

**Decision.**

Stage 6 operations reload pre-saved model bundles and structured tables, and checks that the reloaded production models reproduce the Stage 5 free-running hours above 26 °C. Cached stress-test tables are reused exclusively when their timestamps post-date all referenced model bundle files.

**Alternatives considered.**

Re-fitting machine learning surrogate models natively within Stage 6 was evaluated and rejected, as decentralized model estimation undermines pipeline consistency and compromises reproducibility.

**Consequences.**

Stage 6 analytical results remain strictly consistent with preceding workflow stages by construction, and any observed data mismatch serves as a diagnostic indicator of underlying execution run discrepancies.

---

### ADR-040 — Explicit Distinction and Documentation of Dual Model Training Sets

**Context.**

Model Set A (developed in Stage 4 and trained on earlier complete historical years) and Model Set B (developed in Stage 5 and trained on the complete historical record) capture different temporal domains and support distinct analytical objectives.

**Decision.**

A dedicated tracking table records the specific training data parameters, held-out evaluation subsets, and operational uses for each model set. Furthermore, the resulting difference in projected hours above 26 °C between the two sets is explicitly quantified and reported as model-instance spread.

**Alternatives considered.**

Leaving the distinction between evaluation models and full-record production models implicit within the codebase was evaluated and rejected, as it obscures the provenance of reported projection metrics.

**Consequences.**

Empirical validation evidence derived from evaluation subsets and future projection outputs generated from full-record models are kept distinct, preventing analytical confusion.

---

### ADR-041 — Implementation of Uniform-Warming Stress Tests as Surrogate Sensitivity Analyses

**Context.**

Future weather may be warmer than the Design Summer Year files.

**Decision.**

Uniform temperature increments spanning zero, one, two, and three degrees Celsius (`DT_LEVELS = [0, 1, 2, 3]` °C) are added directly to the DSY outdoor time series. Input features are subsequently rebuilt, and the resulting mean indoor thermal response, the gradient of exceedance hours, the magnitude of warming required to reach the Severe overheating tier, and the proportion of hours with outdoor drivers exceeding the training maximum are systematically reported.

**Alternatives considered.**

Attempting to infer directionality and magnitude of out-of-sample error directly from structural tree ensemble splits was evaluated and rejected, as theoretical inference without empirical perturbation is unjustifiable.

**Consequences.**

Projections generated under out-of-sample input conditions are retained but explicitly flagged, ensuring that the sensitivity analysis characterizes the functional response of the surrogate model rather than predicting actual building physics.

### ADR-042 — Comparative Evaluation of Two-Cluster and Three-Cluster Partitioning Structures

**Context.**

With seven monitored rooms, the number of archetypal clusters is not self-evident.

**Decision.**

For cluster configurations of $k = 2$ and $k = 3$, silhouette scores, agreement metrics against Ward hierarchical clustering via Adjusted Rand Index, minimum cluster sizes, and leave-one-room-out stability metrics are calculated and reported alongside bootstrap confidence intervals. The spatial partition demonstrating stability across all evaluation checks is designated as the reference result, while alternative partitions are presented descriptively. The configuration parameter `N_CLUSTERS` defines the archetype labels carried forward into graphical figures.

**Alternatives considered.**

Fixing the number of clusters arbitrarily in advance without empirical stability testing was evaluated and rejected, as structural partitioning must be justified by internal data characteristics.

**Consequences.**

Cluster membership assignments are derived dynamically from current execution runs. Because the clustering procedure relies on projections generated by the primary surrogate model, resulting classifications remain specific to that particular model instantiation.

### ADR-043 — Exclusion of Outdoor SHAP Attribution Share from Cluster Analysis Features

**Context.**

The proportional share of Shapley Additive exPlanations mass attributed to external meteorological features could potentially serve as a quantitative input for spatial zone clustering.

**Decision.**

Outdoor attribution shares are displayed exclusively as visual overlays rather than functioning as clustering input features. This exclusion is justified because outdoor attribution proportions exhibit minimal variance across rooms—being heavily dominated by the indoor-lag term—thereby reducing overall cluster separation capability.

**Alternatives considered.**

Including outdoor SHAP attribution share as an active feature within the clustering algorithm was evaluated and rejected due to its limited discriminatory power across individual thermal zones.

**Consequences.**

Spatial clustering procedures rely strictly on direct performance and thermal indicators, comprising projected hours above 26 °C, cooling degree-hours above 24 °C, peak indoor temperature, and the thermal gain from the warming stress test.

---

### ADR-044 — Deployment of an Unweighted Room Vulnerability Index Restricted to Cluster Labelling

**Context.**

Combining multiple disparate thermal performance indicators is necessary to establish descriptive labels for identified spatial clusters.

**Decision.**

The Room Vulnerability Index is formulated as the arithmetic mean of min-max normalized clustering features and is utilised solely for ordering and naming cluster archetypes.

**Alternatives considered.**

Applying expert-derived or machine-learned weighting coefficients across vulnerability indicators was evaluated and rejected, as arbitrary weighting schemes are difficult to audit when applied across a seven-zone residential case study.

**Consequences.**

The vulnerability index maintains complete mathematical transparency but carries no implicit value judgment regarding the relative importance of individual overheating indicators.

---

### ADR-045 — Contextualisation of Overheating Screening Tiers with Reliability Metrics and Sensitivity Audits

**Context.**

Relying solely on categorical overheating exposure tiers invites over-interpretation, particularly in thermal zones where surrogate model predictive reliability is reduced.

**Decision.**

Cooling-season exposure hours exceeding 26 °C are classified into study-specific screening tiers comprising Low (≤ 1%), Elevated (> 1 to 3%), High (> 3 to 10%), and Severe (> 10%), with upper bounds inclusive. Tiers are consistently presented alongside hot-hour confidence flags—categorized as high, medium, or low based on mean top-decile heatwave absolute bias thresholds of less than 0.5 K, between 0.5 and 1.5 K, and greater than or equal to 1.5 K, respectively—along with quantified model-instance spread. Furthermore, screening tiers are recomputed across all alternative surrogate model formulations and split-conformal uncertainty bounds, documenting the exact count of unchanged room-scenario-horizon cases and associated rank-order correlations against the primary surrogate.

**Alternatives considered.**

Reporting categorical overheating exposure tiers in isolation without accompanying reliability context or sensitivity checks was evaluated and rejected, as standalone exposure tiers conceal underlying model uncertainty.

**Consequences.**

Overheating screening tiers function strictly as screening indicators to highlight zones requiring advanced investigation, rather than representing definitive classifications of building thermal performance.
